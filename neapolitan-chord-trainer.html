How a Neapolitan (♭II) chord is built

A Neapolitan (♭II) chord — written ♭II (e.g. C♭II) — is a 3-note chord: a plain major triad (Lesson 8), but rooted a minor 2nd (half step) above the tonic instead of on it. Every ♭II chord — in any of the 12 keys — is built the exact same way, by counting semitones to the right from a root note:

  1. Start from the tonic you select — the Neapolitan chord is built relative to it, not on it.
  2. Count 1 semitone up from the tonic — the ♭2nd degree, which becomes the root of this chord (hence "♭II").
  3. Count 4 more semitones up from that root (5 semitones from the tonic) — the major 3rd of the ♭II chord.
  4. Count 3 more semitones up from the 3rd (8 semitones from the tonic) — the perfect 5th of the ♭II chord, completing a plain major triad (Lesson 8) built a half step above the tonic.
Example — C♭II: start on D♭, count 4 semitones right to reach F, count 3 semitones more to reach A♭. D♭‑F‑A♭ is the C♭II chord.
A Neapolitan chord borrows a major triad from a half step above the tonic — a surprising chromatic color in both classical and gospel harmony. It typically functions like a substitute subdominant, most often moving ♭II → V → I, exactly like the progressions in this lesson.

Every chord is played as a full 3-note Neapolitan (♭II) chord (♭ii (chord root), major 3rd of ♭ii, perfect 5th of ♭ii together).
